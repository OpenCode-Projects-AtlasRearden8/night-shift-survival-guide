# Family-boundary scripts

Night shift only works when the household knows the schedule as well as you do.
The failure mode is not conflict; it is *ambiguity*: a spouse trying to be kind,
a parent "just checking in," a well-meaning neighbor ringing the doorbell at
11 a.m. Everyone acted reasonably. You still slept 3 hours. Fix the ambiguity —
out loud, in words, in advance — and most of the problem dissolves.

Scripts below are copy-paste friendly. Fill in the blanks, change words to sound
like you, and put them into your own texts. Rehearsed-and-said-out-loud beats
perfect every time.

## Step 1: the household meeting (5 minutes, once)

> "I'm on nights for [X weeks / ongoing]. My sleep window is **[start] to
> [end]**, every day. I'll keep the anchor block on days off too. When I'm
> sleeping, treat it like I'm at work: emergencies, wake me. Everything else,
> it can wait for [end time] — and I promise it will actually get handled
> then."

Two extras that make this real:

* **Put the window where people see it.** Fridge whiteboard, family calendar,
  shared phone calendar with the sleep block as an actual (private) event. A
  written window is a boundary; an unwritten one is a mood.
* **Name the exceptions together** — school pickup day? Doctor's appointment at
  1 p.m.? Decide exceptions *in advance* so a surprise never has to be argued
  at 11:40 a.m.

## Script: partner

The kindest thing a partner can be told is exactly, boringly, clearly:

> "The two things that wreck my sleep are noise past [start] and questions
> before [end]. If something's on fire, wake me. Otherwise, anything before
> [end] — school forms, dinner plans, the garage — gets a sticky note or a
> text. I'll handle it all at [end]. I will be more human after [end]. And
> thank you for carrying dinner chaos alone this stretch."

And to be fair to you on days off:

> "On my days off I still keep my anchor block ([start]–[end]), so if you see
> me sleeping at noon on a Tuesday, that's the plan working, not me being
> lazy."

## Script: kids (age-calibrated)

**Little kids (concrete, visual):**

> "See this moon on the door? When the moon is up, Mom/Dad is sleeping like a
> superhero. If it's an EMERGENCY — you're hurt, or something is broken or on
> fire — come get me. Otherwise it goes on my list for when the moon comes
> down."

Keep an emergency criteria list at kid height. Kids are extremely good at
respecting a rule they helped define: let them color the moon sign themselves.

**Teens (respect + honesty):**

> "You don't need to tiptoe, but headphones over speakers past [start], and
> don't slam the microwave. If a friend calls, 'they're asleep' is fine — it's
> true. I'll text them back at [end]. If it's a real emergency, wake me."

Teens also handle "I'm not mad, I'm tired" very well when it's said before the
grumpy 11 a.m. moment, not during one.

## Script: parents / in-laws

Grandparents mean well and will call at 10 a.m. forever unless told otherwise:

> "Hi! Quick update so you don't worry if I don't answer: I work nights now.
> I sleep from [start] to [end]. Call twice in a row if it's an emergency and
> I'll pick up. Otherwise I'll call you back at [end]."

The "call twice" convention is gold: it turns any phone into an emergency-
priority system with zero wrangling.

## Script: the group chat / friends

> "FYI I'm on nights: asleep ~[start]–[end]. Non-urgent texts get answered at
> [end]. If I seem to have vanished for a couple days mid-stretch, I'm not
> ignoring you — I'm sleeping, then working, then sleeping again. I'll surface
> on [day off]."

## Script: doorbell, delivery, and the neighbor across the hall

* **Sign for the door:** "Night shift worker sleeping 9–2. Please leave
  packages at door. No rings/knocks (emergencies: phone). Thank you!" A
  laminated sign is fully socially acceptable and works startlingly well.
* **The neighbor, in person or taped to the door with a coffee as a bribe:**

> "Hey! I work nights, so I sleep mornings. If your lawn guy / contractor /
  deliveries can swing by after 2 p.m., that would save my sleep and I'll owe
  you cookies. Happy to talk about schedules if there's a middle ground!"

If the neighbor's regular contractor can't start later and earplugs don't
help much, this becomes an environment problem (see the sleep environment
chapter) plus optionally a landlord / homeowners-association question.
Escalate with paperwork, not with 2 p.m. screaming.

## Script: day-shift coworkers and scheduling

The most overlooked boundary is at work:

> "Can we move the hammering-out-of-scheduling stuff for night staff to
> [time]? I just got off a 12 and I'm about to drive."

> "If anything really needs me same-day, call — twice in a row — after that
> I'm asleep. Email waits for my evening like a good boy."

Also: decline the "join the day-shift lunch meeting" by proposing it on your
evening instead. You're not being difficult; you're being available at the
hour that actually exists for you.

## Script: you, to yourself

One for the mirror, because boundaries fail inward first:

> "Sleeping is my job right now. [Start] to [end] is not optional slack — it
> is the thing that makes the *other* 18 hours work. Chores, texts, guilt,
> and the fantasy of a 'productive day' all move to [end]."

## Handling pushback without a fight

When someone pushes back (rare but real: "But Mom, you slept *yesterday*
too..."), stay boring and consistent:

* Repeat the window like a broken record, warmly: "Yep — [start] to [end],
  every day. That's the whole trick."
* Don't debate fairness at 11 a.m. Sleepy you is not your best negotiator.
  Say: "Let's talk at [end] when I'm rested," and then *actually* talk at
  [end] — keeping that tiny promise is what makes the boundary durable.
