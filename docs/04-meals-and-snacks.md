# Meals and snack tactics

Eating on nights is strange by definition: your stomach lives in one timezone and
your schedule lives in another. This chapter is about feeding yourself so that
energy is steady at 2 a.m. and your body can actually sleep at 9 a.m.

## The evening is your morning

The mental flip that makes food make sense: **your evening is daytime.** Treat it
that way:

* **"Breakfast" = your pre-shift meal** (afternoon for many night workers). Make
  it a real meal, protein-forward, the anchor fuel for the whole shift.
* **"Lunch" = the around-midnight meal.** Keep it moderate — a heavy plate at
  1 a.m. is a sleep melter for a lot of people.
* **"Dinner" = the light after-shift snack** before your sleep window. Small,
  easy, not a full sit-down meal.

This mirrors the classic rule from daytime life — big meal for fuel, smaller
meal to wind down — just rotated 12 hours.

## Watch the two clocks: energy clock and sleep clock

Food timing does double duty:

1. **Energy clock:** You want to avoid the 2:30 a.m. crash. The fix isn't more
   food at 2:30; it is steadier fuel earlier — protein + fiber + slow carbs,
   distributed, rather than one vending-machine sugar bomb that crashes later.
2. **Sleep clock:** A big meal right before your sleep window fights the drop in
   core temperature and can trigger reflux when you're horizontal. Note:
   lying-flat reflux and a full stomach both add up. Finish a real meal at
   least a couple hours before sleep; a light snack is fine.

## The cafeteria, vending machine, and breakroom map

Be honest and tactical:

* **Scout your unit's 24/7 food reality in week one.** Know which lines run at
  2 a.m., what's reliably there, what's genuinely terrible.
* **Pack your own.** This is the highest-difference single food change for night
  workers. A soft cooler bag with 2–3 deliberate items beats aiming at vending
  machines between call bells.
* **The donut/cookie/pancake-breath trap.**unit birthdays, pharma tables, a
  family bringing baked goods at midnight (it happens constantly in medicine).
  No anti-carb sermon here — just know that sugar at 3 a.m. is a loan with an
  interest payment due at 4:30 a.m., paid to nobody.

## What to actually pack (night-shift-friendly ideas)

Cheap, no-cook-possible, survives a breakroom fridge:

* **Protein anchors:** hard-boiled eggs, Greek yogurt cups, cottage cheese,
  jerky or meat sticks, cheese sticks, leftover rotisserie chicken.
* **Slow carbs:** whole-grain crackers, a good bread, oats in a jar, leftover rice,
  a bag of baby carrots or snap peas.
* **Fruit:** apples, bananas, oranges, grapes (grapes: breakroom currency;
  excellent for bartering silence from coworkers).
* **The warm meal plan:** one thermos of soup or chili is elite night food —
  warm, small, forgiving at 1 a.m. If your hospital has a microwave and a decent
  soup mug, the thermos life is worth trying for a week.
* **Hydration station:** a water bottle always there — small repeated sips beat
  one chug at 4 a.m. The catch: a full bladder at 5 a.m. is a schedule disruptor
  on your sleep window too. Front-load water in the first half of the shift,
  taper on the back half.

## Rough "plate" guide for the midnight meal

Keep it boringly simple — protein, some fiber, some carbs, not enormous:

| Slot | What it's for | Example plate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pre-shift meal (your "breakfast") | All-night fuel | Rice bowl w/ chicken + veg, or big turkey sandwich + fruit |
| Midnight meal (your "lunch") | Steady, moderate | Soup + yogurt + crackers; or half-sandwich + apple |
| Post-shift snack (your "dinner") | Light and quick | Yogurt + banana; toast + peanut butter; cottage cheese + fruit |
| Sleep window | None | Finish eating before winding down |

## Everybody is different (digestion edition)

Some people genuinely do fine eating a bigger meal at 2 a.m.; others get reflux
from midnight salad. Ways to learn your own pattern:

1. Keep one week of "what I ate / how I slept" notes (2 lines a night).
2. Change *one* thing at a time — e.g., move the midnight meal an hour earlier.
3. Compare weeks. That's it. That's the whole science; it's cheap and it's yours.

If night eating consistently wrecks your stomach or sleep — heartburn that shows
up regularly, nausea on nights, etc. — that's worth a conversation with your own
provider, since persistent GI symptoms deserve real evaluation, not just schedule
tweaks.
